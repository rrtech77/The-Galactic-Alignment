✦ Apocatastasis

Restoration — first in Aristotle (4th cent. BC); cosmological, astrological, cyclical. The return of all things to their natural state. In Gnostic thought, Sophia becomes re-integrated into the Pleroma. Equivalent to resurrection: the restoration of Man with God and the recovery of purity.

Apocalypse means revelation, usually reserved for revelations mediated or explained by supernatural beings (usually angels). They disclose a transcendental world of supernatural powers and eschatological scenarios that includes judgement of the dead.

Apokatastasis appears in Aristotle's writings in the 4th cent. BC and means restoration of being to its natural state. During the Hellenistic period it developed a cosmological, astrological and medical meaning (resetting of a joint). It could also mean re-establishment of civil peace or individual rights. In Philo Judaeus the 'perfect apocatastasis of the soul' confirms philosophical healing.

The Stoics refined the idea and identified the Great Year with the sidereal Great Year which scheduled the destruction of the world (ekpyrosis) either by a flood or conflagration, followed by its regeneration (palingenesis).

The Precession of the Equinoxes

Earth's axial precession — the ~26,000 year wobble cycle
The Great Year — four precessional seasons of the zodiacal wheel

Precession of the Equinox is the observed phenomenon whereby the sign of the Zodiac rising on the dawn of the vernal equinox (equinoctial point) moves backward (precesses) through the constellations of the Zodiac. General precession is said to be the sum of the lunisolar (the combined gravitational effects of the sun, moon) and the planetary components acting on the Earth's equatorial bulge that causes the Earth's polar axis to wobble clockwise in a slow circle (precess), like the motion of a spinning top. To an observer on Earth the point of equinox changes at about 50.29 arc seconds annually (one degree every 71.5 years). At this rate the entire precession cycle time required to traverse all twelve constellations of the ancient Zodiac, is 25,770 years, although evidence indicates it is declining. The precessional cycle is also called the Great Year or Platonic or Plato's year and is divided into twelve ages with four seasons (the signs at the 4 quarters of the zodiacal wheel shown above, right).

Precession alters the position of the stars, constellations and the Galactic Plane of our Milky Way such that the sun's positions on the equinoxes or solstices periodically align with the Milky Way. The precession of the equinoxes (which also could be called the precession of the solstices) was believed to be responsible for a succession of World Ages.

The accepted theory — lunisolar precession — holds that combined gravitational effects of the Sun and Moon on the Earth's equatorial bulge cause the polar axis to wobble clockwise in a slow circle, like a spinning top. The alternative view favoured here is that our Sun orbits in a binary system with a companion star (possibly an invisible brown dwarf) over a 26,000-year period. In this model the whole solar system's orientation to the fixed stars rotates, with no gyroscopic wobble required.

Reckoned by temperature. Used in Rome, Denmark, former USSR, Australia. Northern hemisphere: spring Mar 1, summer Jun 1, autumn Sep 1, winter Dec 1.

Begins at solstices and equinoxes. Cross-quarter days are midpoints. Used in the US calendar: winter Dec 21, spring Mar 20, summer Jun 20, autumn Sep 22.

Version 1 — Solar Calendar

The Mayan Haab consisted of 18 months of 20 days plus a short month of 5 days at the winter solstice: 18 × 20 + 5 = 365 days. Every 20th year, 5 extra days were added to the short month: 365 × 20 + 5 = 7,305 days ÷ 20 = 365.25 days (equivalent to the leap year concept). On the 400th year only 2 extra days were added instead of 5, yielding: 19 × 7,305 + 7,302 = 146,097 ÷ 400 = 365.2425 days — identical to the Gregorian calendar.

Version 2 — Lunar Cycle

A perfect repeatable lunar cycle of 235 moons in 19 years — already demonstrated in Mayan glyphs at Copan — is equivalent to the Metonic cycle, independently discovered by Babylonians and Greeks. According to the Mayan ratio of 149 moons equalling 4,400 days: 4,400 ÷ 149 = 29.5302 days per lunation. Multiplied by 235 moons = 6,939.597 days ÷ 19 years = 365.2420 days — more accurate than the Gregorian calendar.

Mayans do not consider themselves polytheists. Their supreme deity is Ahau — analogous to Jehovah or Allah as creator of everything. There were thirteen gods of the thirteen heavens and nine gods of the nine underworlds. Between the upper heavens and the underworlds of the night and death was the earthly plane — depicted in art as a two-headed turtle lying in a great lake.

The Mayan Great Chain of Being as described in the Popol Vuh includes: Ahau (Heart of Heaven and Heart of the Earth, creator of all); three Lightning Gods forming a trinity (together making up the Heart of Heaven); nine Creator-Formers who fashioned the first humans from nine drinks of maize gruel (two earlier human-like races — of mud, then of wood — had been destroyed); and the Four Cardinal Directions.

Tepeu and Gukumatz (also known as Kukulcan, or the Aztec Quetzalcoatl) were two of the first beings to exist — as wise as sages — and are called the Creators and the Forefathers. Huracan (Heart of Heaven) acted as a storm god doing the actual creating while Tepeu and Gucumatz guided the process. Mayan divinities are not discrete but merge into each other.

The Myth of Hun-Hunahpu

According to the Popol Vuh, Hun-Hunahpu ("One-Hunahpu," a calendrical name) is the father of the Maya Hero Twins — Hunahpu and Ixbalanque — by a virgin. Paired with his brother Vucub-Hunahpu, Hun-Hunahpu was defeated in the ballgame by the lords of the Underworld (Xibalba) and sacrificed; his head was suspended in a trophy-tree where it changed to a calabash whose spittle impregnated Xquic, a daughter of a Xibalba lord. She fled the underworld and conceived the Twins. After the Twins defeated the lords of Xibalba, they recovered the remains of their father and uncle but could not resuscitate them.

It has been asserted that the Classic Period Maya believed Hun-Hunahpu to have been reborn as maize. The scene of the Tonsured Maize God rising from a turtle carapace (the "tomb" of the earth) is interpreted as Hun-Hunahpu resurrected, flanked by the Hero Twins as the maize deity's sons. A male maize deity (God E) is present in all three extant Maya books; the Classic period distinguished two male forms: a Foliated and a Tonsured Maize God.

Scholarly Caveat The identification of Hun-Hunahpu with the Maize Deity remains a theory in need of corroboration. The Popol Vuh itself does not unequivocally state Hun-Hunahpu to be, or become, identical with the maize, nor is he resurrected by his sons. The hieroglyphical name of the Tonsured Maize God — possibly "Hun-Nal-Ye" — is not recognisable as that of Hun-Hunahpu. Even assuming the Twins are "resurrecting" the main maize god, there is no compelling reason to identify him as Hun-Hunahpu.

Pacal's Sarcophagus and the Sacred Tree

Jenkins interprets the famous sarcophagus lid of Palenque ruler Pacal as showing the Sacred Tree as the doorway to the underworld — the crossing point of the Milky Way and ecliptic. The Milky Way tree serves as an extension of Pacal's umbilicus, his entrance into life and into death. Lord (Ahau) Pacal, equated with the sun by divine kingship, is portrayed "entering" the Sacred Tree.

"The day when the sun conjuncts the crossing point of ecliptic and Milky Way — when the Milky Way arches overhead from the region of Polaris (Heart of Sky) — is the only date when the Sun/Lord could jump from the ecliptic track and travel the Milky Way up and around the vault of heaven to the region of Polaris, there to enter the 'Heart of Sky.' This date changes with precession." — John Major Jenkins

The dark bifurcation in the Milky Way — caused by interstellar dust clouds — appears as a dark road beginning near the ecliptic and stretching along the Milky Way toward Polaris. The Quiché Maya call it xibalba be (the "road to Xibalba"); the Chorti call it the "camino de Santiago"; the ancient Maya called it the "Black Road" in the Popol Vuh. The Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque must journey down this road to battle the Lords of Xibalba.

The Mayan World Tree — symbolic of the creation and ordering of the world, the axis of Earth and Sky with roots in Xibalba and branches reaching the heavens — is called Wakah Chan ("raised up sky") in the Temple of the Cross at Palenque. Atop it sits Itzam-Yeh, the "principal bird deity," possibly representing the zenith or the pivot of the heavens about the North Star. On Pacal's sarcophagus lid, the dead king is depicted falling along the World Tree into Xibalba; an accompanying text reports that he has "taken the road" — the Sak be, or white road, a name for the Milky Way.

Jenkins speaks of "the dark rift" — a black ridge along the Milky Way caused by interstellar dust clouds — known to the ancient Maya as xibalba be ("road to the Underworld"). Xibalba, roughly translated as "Place of Fear," is the Mayan underworld, ruled by spirits of disease and death. In 16th-century Verapaz, the entrance was traditionally a cave near Cobán, Guatemala. Another physical incarnation is the dark rift visible in the Milky Way.

Xibalba is described in the Popol Vuh as a court below the Earth's surface, ruled by twelve lords: the first two were One Death and Seven Death. The remaining ten are given commission over various forms of human suffering — sickness, starvation, fear, destitution, pain, and death. Xibalba was a great city, but rife with tests, trials, and traps.

Xibalba was also home to a famous ballcourt where the heroes of the Popol Vuh first succumbed to the trickery of the Xibalbans — a deadly bladed ball — and then returned as the Hero Twins to outwit them. The Xibalbans once received human sacrifice from those on the surface; the Twins tricked them into accepting counterfeit sacrifices, then humiliated them into accepting lesser offerings. Their role after defeat remains unclear, though Xibalba continued as a dark underworld place long after.

The Cuatro Flores is the prototype for the modern Chiapas cross found in San Juan de Chamula and elsewhere among the Maya of today. The Maya appropriated this Christian symbol, merging it with the World Tree of the Center, the yax che'il kab. The Christian cross became the pivot and pillar of their cosmos, just as the World Tree had been before.

The La Cruz de las Cuatro Flores (The Cross of the Four Flowers), the first Indian-Christian cross of the Americas is attributed to an unknown Mesoamerican artist presumed active in Puebla circa 1530s-1540s of the early post-Conquest period. The intricately carved wood cross has merged indigenous motifs and forms of the Yukatec Maya, Mixtec, and Aztec traditions into the Spanish-Christian tradition.

In the Temple of the Foliated Cross, the World Tree is the Na Te K'an (First Precious Tree), a symbol of the sky-raising Maize God Hun-Nal-Ye.

The first Europeans who saw the images of the World Tree at Palenque called the buildings housing them the Temple of the Cross and the Temple of the Foliated Cross, since they had the same shape as images in Christian churches. The carvings of these ancient trees are outlined with reflective mirrors and they wear jade necklaces and loincloths as if they were living beings. Modern Christian-Maya crosses both in Yukatan and Chiapas are decorated with mirrors and dressed in clothing, or flowers and pine boughs. They too are considered to be living beings.

The name of the tree [at Palenque] literally meant 'raised-up sky'. The Classic texts at Palenque tell us that the central axis of the cosmos was called the 'raised-up sky' because First Father had raised it at the beginning of creation in order to separate the sky from the earth. Each world tree was, therefore, a representation of the axis of creation.

In Yucatec, the term yaxche signifies first or green tree. In ancient Maya religion, green is the color associated with the central place. On page 1 of the Codex Fejervary-Mayer, the four directional trees appear with their accompanying birds, gods, days, and Yearbearers.

For Maya inhabitants of Santiago Atitlan, Guatemala, the church represents a microcosm of the universe. In the center of the church's nave is a small hole called the R'muxux Ruchiliew (meaning "navel of the earth") which traditionalists conceive as a portal leading to the underworld realm of their sacred ancestors. The navel hole is only uncovered once a year at midnight prior to Holy Friday during Easter Week. Later that morning, the Atitecos lower into the hole a massive wooden cross bearing a life-size sculpture of Christ crucified. The placement of the cross of Christ in the earth signifies not only his entrance into the underworld in death, but also represents the means by which the resurrected God reemerges to new life from the regenerative center of creation.

The Mam figure (meaning "grandfather, or ancient one") symbolically oversees the death of Christ and his descent into the underworld. The maize god and Jesus Christ represent analogous figures who emerge from the underworld to bring life and charge the cosmos with renewed sacred power.

Maya cosmology describes the emergence of life centered at a great body of water from which three mountains grew under the direction of the gods at the time of creation. The emergence of three great mountains from the primordial sea parallels the myth of Hun-Nal-Ye, the god of maize. Today many Maya still have three-stone hearths in the center of their houses.
